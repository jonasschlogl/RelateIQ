You plan a thoughtful surprise. Your partner says thanks, but seems more excited when you simply sit with them on the sofa and put your phone away. Or you tell them every day how much you love them, and they say they'd rather you just helped with the dishes.

That gap is why the idea of love languages became so popular. Gary Chapman's book has sold millions of copies, and "what's your love language?" is now a normal first-date question. But in the last few years, relationship scientists have looked closely at the idea, and the picture is more interesting than a five-option quiz.

This guide explains the five love languages, what the research actually shows, and how to use the idea in a way that genuinely brings you closer.

## What are the 5 love languages?

The idea is that people tend to show and feel love in different ways. Chapman described five:

| Love language | What it looks like | Small example |
|---|---|---|
| Words of affirmation | Saying what you appreciate and love | "I'm really proud of how you handled today." |
| Quality time | Full attention, together | A walk with phones in your pockets |
| Receiving gifts | Thoughtful things that say "I thought of you" | Their favourite snack after a long day |
| Acts of service | Doing things that make life easier | Handling the call they've been dreading |
| Physical touch | Affection through closeness | A hug when you get home |

Most people recognise themselves in more than one row. That turns out to matter.

## What the research actually says

Two studies give a useful, balanced picture.

In 2022, Olha Mostova, Maciej Stolarski and Gerald Matthews studied 100 couples. People whose partners expressed love in the way they preferred to receive it were more satisfied with their relationship and their sex life. Interestingly, being more empathetic didn't automatically make someone better at "speaking" their partner's language. It seemed to take attention and effort, not just a kind personality.

Then in 2024, Emily Impett, Gideon Park and Amy Muise reviewed the research more broadly. They found little support for Chapman's main claims: that everyone has one primary love language, that there are exactly five, and that couples need to match. When people rate each type of affection instead of being forced to choose, they tend to rate all of them as important. As Impett put it, people are basically happier when they receive any of these expressions of love.

Their suggestion is a "balanced diet" approach. A relationship needs a range of nutrients, including the five love languages, but also things the original list leaves out, like supporting each other's goals, companionship and emotional support.

> **Why it works this way**
> The love languages quiz forces you to choose: words or time, gifts or touch. Real relationships don't work like that. Most of us need several kinds of care, and what we need changes with stress, season and stage of life. A tired new parent may crave acts of service, then quality time once the baby sleeps through the night. Thinking of love languages as a menu rather than a label keeps you curious about what your partner needs right now, instead of assuming you already know.

## How to actually use love languages

### 1. Treat it as a conversation starter, not a test result

The value isn't the label. It's the conversation. Ask each other what made you feel loved recently, and what you'd like more of.

Try: "What's something I did this month that made you feel really cared for?"

### 2. Notice how your partner shows love

People often give love the way they'd like to receive it. A partner who always fixes things, books dinners or sends you articles may be showing care in their own language. Naming it helps: "I've noticed you always sort the car for me. That really does feel like love."

### 3. Ask what they need right now

Needs shift. After a hard week, someone who usually loves words may just want help with dinner.

Try: "This week has been a lot. What would help you feel most supported, some time together or me taking a few things off your plate?"

> **Example: the dishes and the compliments**
> Mia tells Jake she loves him several times a day and sends him sweet messages. Jake loves her, but often feels quietly overwhelmed by chores. One evening Mia asks what would make him feel most loved this week. He says, a little embarrassed, "Honestly, if you cooked twice this week so I could rest." She does. Jake later tells her it was the most loved he'd felt in months.
>
> Nothing was wrong with Mia's words. Jake simply needed a different "nutrient" at that moment.

### 4. Don't keep score

Love languages can turn into a list of demands: "you're not speaking my language". That misses the point. The goal is to understand each other better, not to grade each other.

### 5. Add what the list leaves out

Support for each other's dreams, laughing together, being reliable and being kind during conflict all matter enormously. Our guide to [green flags in a relationship](/blog/green-flags-in-a-relationship/) covers many of them.

### 6. Combine languages

Many of the best moments mix several at once: a walk where you say what you appreciate, or cooking their favourite meal and eating together without phones. You don't have to pick one.

## What most articles miss

Most articles treat love languages as a personality type you discover once and keep forever. The research suggests a more useful view: everyone benefits from all kinds of care, and the real skill is noticing what your partner needs today.

There's also a quiet trap. If you decide your partner "only" speaks one language, you might stop noticing the love they show in other ways. It's worth checking: is your partner really not loving you, or loving you in a way you haven't learned to see yet?

## Love languages during the holidays

The holidays are full of love language moments, and full of mismatches. One partner spends weeks finding the perfect gift, the other just wants an evening together without family. Talk about it early.

Try: "What would make this holiday feel special for you? A big gift, a quiet day together, or something else?"

## Quick answers

### What are the 5 love languages?

Words of affirmation, quality time, receiving gifts, acts of service and physical touch. They describe different ways people tend to show and feel love.

### Are love languages scientifically proven?

Partly. One study found that couples who receive love in their preferred way are more satisfied. But a 2024 review found little support for the idea that everyone has one main language or that couples need to match.

### Can you have more than one love language?

Yes, and most people do. When people rate each type instead of choosing one, they usually rate several as important.

### Can your love language change?

It can. What you need often depends on stress, life stage and what's missing at the moment.

### What if my partner and I have different love languages?

That's very common and not a problem. What matters is that you're both willing to learn what helps the other feel loved, and to try.

### How do I find out my partner's love language?

Watch how they show love, notice what they ask for or complain about, and simply ask what made them feel cared for recently.

### Is it okay to ask for what I need?

Yes. Clear, kind requests are much easier for a partner than hints. "It would mean a lot if we had one evening this week without phones" is a gift to both of you.
