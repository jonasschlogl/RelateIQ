Most relationship advice sounds obvious until you're in the middle of an argument. Then the obvious things — stay calm, listen, don't say the thing you'll regret — are the hardest to do. The good news is that communicating in a relationship is a skill, and skills improve with small, specific habits rather than big resolutions.

Here are seven that come up again and again in the research and methods therapists use, including the Gottman Method and Nonviolent Communication.

## 1. Start soft

How a conversation begins usually decides how it ends. Researchers John and Julie Gottman found that a harsh opening ("You never help with anything") very rarely leads anywhere good. A *softened start-up* does the opposite: it names how *you* feel and what you'd like, without an accusation.

Instead of "You always ignore me when you're on your phone," try "I've been missing time with you lately. Could we put our phones away after dinner?"

## 2. Describe, don't diagnose

"You're being selfish" is a verdict. "You said you'd be home at six and it's now eight" is an observation. Nonviolent Communication (NVC) starts with observations because it's hard to argue with a fact, and very easy to argue with a label.

## 3. Name the need underneath

Behind most complaints is an unmet need: to feel respected, supported, close, or free. "I'm furious that the dishes are still there" often means "I need to know I'm not carrying this alone." Saying the need out loud gives your partner something they can actually respond to.

## 4. Make one clear request

Complaints are easy to wave away; requests are easy to answer. Replace "You need to be more present" with "Can we have thirty minutes without screens tonight?" A good request is specific, doable and positive — it says what you'd like, not what you want to stop.

## 5. Listen to understand, not to reply

Try this in a tense moment: before you respond, repeat back what you heard in your own words. "So you're feeling overwhelmed because you've had two weeks of late work, and the chores felt like one more demand — is that right?" You don't have to agree. You only have to show that you understood. Most arguments soften when both people feel heard.

## 6. Take a break when you're flooded

When your heart is pounding and your thoughts are racing, you are physiologically unable to have a productive conversation. The Gottmans call this *flooding*. The fix isn't to push through; it's to pause for at least twenty minutes, do something calming, and then come back. The key is to say you're coming back: "I need twenty minutes to calm down, and then I want to finish this."

## 7. Repair early and often

Even couples who communicate well say the wrong thing. What separates them is the *repair attempt*: a joke at the right moment, a gentle touch, "Can we start that over?" or a simple "I'm sorry, that came out wrong." Repairs work best when they happen quickly — and when the other person accepts them.

## Where to start

Don't try all seven at once. Pick one — the soft start is a good first choice — and use it for a week. When you want to practise before a real conversation, writing out what you plan to say, or rehearsing it out loud, can make a surprising difference.

If your conversations regularly become frightening, cruel or controlling, that is a different situation from ordinary conflict. In that case, please reach out to a professional or a support service in your country.
