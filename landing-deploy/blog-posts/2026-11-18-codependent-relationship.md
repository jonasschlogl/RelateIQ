You know their mood before they walk in the door. You cancel plans if they seem down. You fix, soothe and manage, and you feel guilty when you don't. People call you caring, and you are. But lately you've started to wonder where you went.

That pattern is often called codependency. It's one of the most searched relationship terms, partly because it's so common and partly because it can be hard to see from the inside. This guide explains what a codependent relationship is, the signs, where it tends to come from, and how to care for someone without losing yourself.

## What is a codependent relationship?

Codependency isn't an official diagnosis, and researchers still debate exactly how to define it. But most descriptions share a few elements: focusing on others more than yourself, sacrificing your own needs, trying to control or fix others, and holding back your own feelings.

In a codependent relationship, one or both partners build their sense of worth around being needed. Caring becomes a way to feel safe, and stepping back feels frightening.

| Healthy support | Codependent pattern |
|---|---|
| You help, and you also look after yourself | You help at the cost of your own health |
| You can say no without heavy guilt | Saying no feels impossible |
| Their feelings matter, and so do yours | Their mood decides your mood |
| You let them handle their own problems | You feel you must fix everything |
| You have a life outside the relationship | The relationship is your whole identity |

## 8 signs of a codependent relationship

### 1. Your mood depends on theirs

If they're happy, you relax. If they're upset, you can't settle until it's fixed.

### 2. You struggle to say no

Even small requests feel like tests you can't fail.

### 3. You feel responsible for their problems

Their work stress, their family conflict, their bad habits all feel like yours to solve.

### 4. You hide your own needs

You'd rather go without than risk being a burden.

### 5. You stay in hurtful situations

You tolerate behaviour you know isn't okay, because leaving or confronting feels worse.

### 6. You feel guilty taking time for yourself

A night out with friends or an hour alone feels selfish.

### 7. You lose touch with what you want

Asked what you'd like to do, you genuinely don't know, or you wait to hear what they want.

### 8. You feel tired and resentful

Underneath the caring, there's exhaustion, and sometimes quiet anger that nobody is caring for you.

## Where codependency often comes from

In a study of people who identified as codependent, researchers Ingrid Bacon, Elizabeth McKay, Frances Reynolds and Anne McIntyre found that participants often linked it to childhood: families with a lot of control, criticism or perfectionism, and a parent who was emotionally or physically absent. Several described a "chameleon-like" self, constantly adapting to others until they lost a clear sense of who they were.

The study was small, so it can't prove causes. But it matches what many people describe: learning early that being useful or easy to be around was the safest way to be loved. It's also closely related to an anxious [attachment style](/blog/how-attachment-styles-work-in-relationships/).

> **Why it works this way**
> If love once felt conditional, your nervous system learns a rule: keep others happy and you'll stay safe. As an adult, that rule keeps running, even with a partner who would love you anyway. Caring isn't the problem. The problem is when caring becomes the only way you feel worthy, so you can't stop, even when it's hurting you.

## How to care without losing yourself

### 1. Notice the urge before you act

When you feel the pull to fix, rescue or smooth things over, pause. Ask: is this mine to fix? What would happen if I didn't?

### 2. Practise small "no"s

Start with low-stakes situations.

Try: "I can't do that tonight, but I could help on Saturday."

It will feel uncomfortable. That discomfort is the old rule being challenged, not proof you've done something wrong.

### 3. Name your own needs out loud

Try: "I've realised I've been putting my needs last for a while. I'd love it if we could plan one evening a week that's just for me to do my own thing."

> **Example: the Friday rescue**
> Every Friday, Anna's partner James came home stressed from work, and Anna dropped everything to cheer him up, cancelling her evening class twice a month. She started to feel resentful. With a counsellor's help, she told him: "I love helping you unwind, but I need my Friday class too. Could we do a proper catch-up on Saturday mornings instead?" James was surprised she'd been cancelling. He'd assumed she wanted to. The change was small, but Anna felt more like herself again.

### 4. Rebuild a life of your own

Hobbies, friends and goals that are just yours help you remember who you are outside the relationship.

### 5. Let your partner handle their own feelings

You can be supportive without taking responsibility for their emotions. "That sounds really hard. What do you think you'll do?" supports without taking over. Our guide on [how to support your partner when they're stressed](/blog/how-to-support-your-partner-when-stressed/) shows the difference.

### 6. Get support

Therapy, especially approaches that look at attachment and boundaries, can help a lot. Support groups such as Co-Dependents Anonymous exist in many countries too.

## What most articles miss

Codependency is often described as a personal flaw, as if caring too much is the problem. But caring is a strength. The goal isn't to care less. It's to include yourself in the circle of people you care for.

It's also not always one-sided. Sometimes both partners have fallen into roles, one as the helper and one as the helped. Changing the pattern can feel destabilising for both, at first. That's normal, and it's often the start of a more equal, more honest relationship.

## Quick answers

### What is a codependent relationship?

A relationship where one or both partners build their sense of worth around being needed, often sacrificing their own needs to care for or control the other.

### What are the signs of codependency?

Your mood depends on theirs, you struggle to say no, you feel responsible for their problems, hide your needs, stay in hurtful situations and feel guilty about time for yourself.

### Is codependency a mental illness?

It's not an official diagnosis, and researchers still debate the definition. But the patterns are real and can cause a lot of stress.

### Can a codependent relationship be healthy?

It can become healthier if both partners recognise the pattern and work on boundaries, independence and honest communication.

### What causes codependency?

It's often linked to childhood experiences, such as controlling or critical families or an absent parent, and to anxious attachment.

### How do I stop being codependent?

Notice the urge to fix, practise small boundaries, name your own needs, rebuild your own life and consider therapy or a support group.

### Is codependency the same as being supportive?

No. Support includes looking after yourself. Codependency means caring for others at the cost of your own wellbeing.

## A note on safety

Codependency often shows up in relationships with addiction or abuse. If your partner hurts, threatens or controls you, or if you feel unable to leave safely, please reach out to a local helpline or support service. You deserve care too.
