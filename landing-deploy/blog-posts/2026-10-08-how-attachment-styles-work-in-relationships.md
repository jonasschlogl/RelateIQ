You've probably seen the terms before: anxious attachment, avoidant attachment, secure attachment. They show up in memes, podcasts and relationship advice everywhere. Underneath the internet version, though, there's real research, and it's more useful and more hopeful than most of the posts suggest.

This guide explains what attachment styles are, what each one tends to look like in dating and relationships, and how to use the idea without turning it into a label.

## Where attachment theory comes from

Attachment theory started with the work of John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth, who studied how babies bond with caregivers. In 1987, Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver applied the idea to adults. They asked people to choose the description that best fit how they felt and behaved in romantic relationships. They used the same three categories Ainsworth had found in infants: secure, avoidant and anxious (often called anxious-ambivalent).

Later, researcher Kim Bartholomew described adult attachment along two dimensions:

- **Attachment anxiety:** worry that a partner will reject or leave you.
- **Attachment avoidance:** discomfort with closeness and with depending on others.

Combine low and high levels of each and you get four styles.

## The four attachment styles

### Secure attachment

Low anxiety, low avoidance. Secure people are generally comfortable with closeness and with independence. In a conflict they tend to say what they mean, listen and repair. Texting example: "Hey, saw this late. Can't talk now, call you at 8?" Being secure doesn't mean never feeling insecure. It means those feelings pass without taking over.

### Anxious attachment (preoccupied)

High anxiety, low avoidance. People with an anxious attachment style tend to want a lot of closeness and reassurance, and are quick to notice signs of distance. When a partner goes quiet, the thought "did I do something wrong?" arrives fast. They may text several times, check read receipts or need to talk it out immediately. The strength here is care and attentiveness. The challenge is that worry can feel like fact.

### Avoidant attachment (dismissing)

Low anxiety, high avoidance. Avoidant people value independence and tend to feel uncomfortable when a relationship gets very close or emotional. They may pull back when things get intense, reply slowly, or say "I just need some space." It's usually less about not caring and more about feeling safer when they have room to breathe. The challenge is that distance can feel like rejection to a partner.

### Fearful-avoidant attachment (also called fearful)

High anxiety, high avoidance. People in this group both want closeness and fear it. Someone might reach out warmly one day and withdraw the next, often confusing both themselves and their partner. It's often described as "come closer, no, go away." It can be the hardest style to live with, and also one where steady, patient relationships and support from a therapist can help a lot.

## When styles meet: the pursue and withdraw loop

One of the best known patterns in couples research is demand and withdraw: one partner moves toward the other to talk, and the other moves away to cool down. The more one pursues, the more the other retreats, and the more the other retreats, the more the first one pursues. An anxious partner paired with an avoidant partner can fall into this loop very easily. Seeing it as a pattern you both take part in, rather than one person's fault, is often the first step to changing it.

## Is your attachment style fixed?

No, and this is the most important point. Attachment researchers describe security as something that can grow. Adults can move toward a more secure style, often with the help of a supportive, reliable relationship (a partner, a friend or a therapist) and by understanding how past experiences shaped their expectations. Studies that follow couples over time have also found that when one partner becomes more secure, the other tends to shift in the same direction.

A few caveats are worth knowing:

- Attachment style isn't a diagnosis or a personality type. It describes patterns, and most people are a mix.
- Your style can look different with different partners or at different times in life.
- Many internet quizzes are simplified. Researchers measure attachment with questionnaires such as the Experiences in Close Relationships scale, where you rate how much you agree with statements about relationships. A quiz can start a conversation, but it can't label you.
- Avoid "diagnosing" your partner. It's a way to understand yourself and your patterns, not to win arguments.

## How to use this in everyday life

- **If you lean anxious:** Pause before sending a third text. Ask yourself what you need and ask for it directly. Our guide to [overthinking in a relationship](/blog/how-to-stop-overthinking-in-a-relationship/) has steps for this.
- **If you lean avoidant:** If you need space, say so and give a time to return: "I need an hour to think, can we talk at 8?" That small sentence protects both of you.
- **If you're secure:** Stay patient. Your steadiness helps a partner who is anxious or avoidant, but you still get to have needs and limits.
- **If you're fearful-avoidant:** Go slowly, notice the push and pull, and consider support from a therapist trained in attachment work.
