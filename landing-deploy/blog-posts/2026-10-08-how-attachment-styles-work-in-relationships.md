You've probably seen the terms before: anxious attachment, avoidant attachment, secure attachment. They show up in memes, podcasts and TikTok videos about why someone "left you on read". Underneath the internet version there are decades of real research, and it's more useful and more hopeful than most of the posts suggest.

This guide explains where attachment styles come from, what the studies actually found, what each style tends to look like in dating and long-term relationships, and why the pattern you have today isn't a life sentence. It also explains the *why* behind each pattern, because once you understand why your body reacts the way it does, it becomes much easier to change what you do next.

## Where attachment theory comes from

Attachment theory started with British psychiatrist John Bowlby, who wanted to understand why babies separated from their caregivers became so distressed. His answer was that humans are born with an attachment system: a built-in drive to stay close to the people who keep us safe. Psychologist Mary Ainsworth then watched how one-year-olds reacted when their mother left a room and came back. She found consistent patterns. Some babies were upset and then easily comforted (secure). Some acted as if they didn't care (avoidant). Some were upset and hard to settle, clinging and angry at the same time (anxious-ambivalent).

In 1987, Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver asked a bold question: does the same system shape how adults fall in love? They printed a "love quiz" in a local newspaper and asked readers to pick which of three short descriptions best fit their romantic relationships. Of the 620 people whose answers they analysed, about 56% chose the secure description, about 25% the avoidant one and about 19% the anxious one. Those numbers were strikingly close to what Ainsworth had found in babies, and they launched decades of research on adult attachment.

| Study | Who | Secure | Avoidant | Anxious |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hazan & Shaver, newspaper quiz (1987) | 620 adults, ages 14 to 82 | 56% | 25% | 19% |
| Hazan & Shaver, second study (1987) | 108 university students | 56% | 23% | 20% |

Two things are worth knowing about those numbers. First, secure was the most common answer, which surprises many people who only know attachment from the internet. Second, people had to choose one box, but later research found that attachment is better described as two sliding scales than as three or four boxes.

## Two dimensions, four styles

In 1990, researcher Kim Bartholomew described adult attachment along two dimensions, and most researchers still measure it this way today:

- **Attachment anxiety:** how much you worry that a partner will reject you, leave you or not love you as much as you love them.
- **Attachment avoidance:** how uncomfortable you are with closeness, with depending on others and with others depending on you.

Everyone sits somewhere on both scales. Combine low and high levels of each and you get the four styles people talk about.

| Style | Anxiety | Avoidance | A late reply might feel like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Secure | Low | Low | "They're probably busy. I'll hear from them later." |
| Anxious | High | Low | "Did I do something wrong? Should I text again?" |
| Avoidant | Low | High | "Good, a bit of space. I'll reply tomorrow." |
| Fearful-avoidant | High | High | "I want them to reply, and I'm also ready to pull away." |

Researchers usually measure these dimensions with questionnaires such as the Experiences in Close Relationships scale, where you rate statements about how you feel and behave in close relationships. That's very different from a ten-question internet quiz that hands you a label.

> **Why it works this way**
> The attachment system works like a smoke alarm for closeness. When a partner feels far away or unreachable, the alarm goes off, and it asks one question: are you there for me? People higher in anxiety tend to have a sensitive alarm that goes off early and loudly, so they move toward the partner to switch it off: calling, texting, asking for reassurance. People higher in avoidance learned, often very early, that reaching out didn't reliably help, so their system turns the volume down instead: they pull back, get busy and handle it alone.
>
> Neither strategy is stupid. Each one made sense somewhere. They just clash badly when two people use opposite ones at the same moment.

## The four attachment styles in everyday life

### Secure attachment

Low anxiety, low avoidance. Secure people are generally comfortable with both closeness and independence. When something feels off, they tend to ask rather than guess, and in a conflict they can say what they mean, listen and repair.

Being secure doesn't mean never feeling jealous, hurt or insecure. It means those feelings pass without taking over the evening, and that you trust the relationship enough to raise them.

> **Example: a secure partner on a quiet evening**
> Sam texts his girlfriend at 6 pm and gets no reply until 10. He notices a small "huh", checks in once ("Hope your day's okay!") and goes on with his evening. When she replies, explaining the work deadline, he says it's fine and they plan to call the next day.
>
> What makes this secure isn't that Sam felt nothing. It's that the feeling didn't turn into a story about the relationship.

### Anxious attachment (preoccupied)

High anxiety, low avoidance. People with an anxious attachment style want a lot of closeness and reassurance, and they are quick to notice any sign of distance. When a partner goes quiet, the thought "did I do something wrong?" arrives fast. They may text several times, check when the other person was last online, or need to talk it through immediately.

The strength here is real: anxious partners are often warm, attentive and emotionally tuned in. The challenge is that worry can feel exactly like fact, and the behaviour that comes from that worry (more texts, more questions, more checking) can push away the very person they want to feel close to.

> **Example: the three-message evening**
> Lena's boyfriend replies "ok" to a long message about her day. Within an hour she has sent "Is everything alright?", then "You seem off", then "Never mind, forget it." He was at the gym and didn't see any of them. When he finally calls, she's already hurt and he feels accused of something he didn't do.
>
> Try: "Hey, I noticed I got a bit anxious when it went quiet. All good with you?"

### Avoidant attachment (dismissing)

Low anxiety, high avoidance. Avoidant people value independence and tend to feel uncomfortable when a relationship gets very close or emotional. They may pull back when things get intense, change the subject, reply slowly or say "I just need some space."

It's usually less about not caring and more about feeling safer with room to breathe. Many avoidant people care a lot. They just learned that needing someone was risky, so they got very good at not needing anyone. The challenge is that distance feels like rejection to a partner, and silence is easily read as "I don't care."

> **Example: the disappearing weekend**
> After a big, emotional conversation about moving in together, Daniel goes quiet for two days. He isn't angry. He feels overloaded and doesn't know what to say yet, so he says nothing. His partner reads the silence as doubt about the whole relationship.
>
> Try: "That conversation was a lot for me. I want to keep talking about it, I just need until Sunday to think."

### Fearful-avoidant attachment (also called fearful)

High anxiety, high avoidance. People in this group want closeness and fear it at the same time. Someone might reach out warmly one day and withdraw the next, often confusing both themselves and their partner. It's often summed up as "come closer, no, go away."

This can be the hardest style to live with, especially for the person who has it. It's often linked to earlier experiences where the people who were supposed to be safe weren't always safe. Steady, patient relationships and support from a therapist trained in attachment or trauma work can help a lot.

## When styles meet: the pursue and withdraw loop

One of the best studied patterns in couples research is called demand and withdraw. One partner moves toward the other to talk ("We need to sort this out"), and the other moves away to cool down ("I can't do this right now"). The more one pursues, the more the other retreats. The more the other retreats, the more the first one pursues.

This isn't a small thing. In a 2014 meta-analysis, communication researcher Paul Schrodt and his colleagues combined 74 studies with more than 14,000 participants. Couples caught in the demand and withdraw pattern reported lower relationship satisfaction, less intimacy and poorer communication. The pattern was also linked to anxiety and aggression. And it didn't matter much who did the demanding and who did the withdrawing. The loop itself was the problem.

An anxious partner paired with an avoidant partner can fall into this loop very easily, because each person's way of calming down sets off the other person's alarm.

> **Example: Mia and Tom's Sunday argument**
> Mia brings up that Tom has been on his phone all weekend. Tom says "Can we not do this now?" and goes to the kitchen. Mia follows him: "See, this is exactly what I mean." Tom goes quiet. Mia raises her voice. Tom grabs his keys and goes for a drive. Both of them end the day feeling alone, and both believe the other one started it.
>
> What would change the loop: Mia names her need instead of the problem ("I miss you this weekend"), and Tom names his break instead of disappearing ("I'm getting overwhelmed. Give me 20 minutes and I'll come back").

> **Why it works**
> Naming the loop as something you both do takes the blame out of the room. The pursuer can usually soften once they know the withdrawer is coming back, because what scared them was the disappearance, not the break. And the withdrawer can usually come back once they're not being chased, because what overwhelmed them was the pressure, not the topic. A short, specific promise ("20 minutes, then I'm back") answers both alarms at once.

## Is your attachment style fixed?

No, and this is the most important point in this whole guide.

The first study to follow the same people across several decades was published in 2019 by William Chopik and colleagues. They combined five long-running studies covering 628 people, tracked across a span of 59 years between the ages of 13 and 72. Attachment anxiety tended to rise in adolescence and young adulthood and then fall steadily through the rest of life. Attachment avoidance started higher in adolescence and declined slowly over time. At every age, people who were in a close romantic relationship tended to score lower on both anxiety and avoidance.

That doesn't prove that a relationship *causes* someone to become more secure. It might also be that more secure people find it easier to stay in relationships. But it fits what therapists see every day: attachment patterns can shift, especially through reliable relationships, understanding your history and practising new responses when the old alarm goes off. Researchers sometimes call this "earned security".

A few caveats are worth knowing:

- Attachment style isn't a diagnosis or a personality type. It describes patterns, and most people are a mix.
- Your style can look different with different partners or at different times in life. Someone can feel secure with a steady partner and anxious with an unpredictable one.
- Many internet quizzes are simplified. A quiz can start a conversation, but it can't label you.
- Avoid "diagnosing" your partner. Attachment is a way to understand yourself and your patterns, not a way to win arguments.

## Common myths about attachment styles

| Myth | What the research actually suggests |
|---|---|
| "Most people are anxious or avoidant." | In most adult studies, secure is the most common style. |
| "Avoidant people don't love their partners." | Avoidance is about discomfort with dependence, not a lack of feeling. |
| "Anxious means needy or clingy." | Anxiety is a sensitive alarm. Many anxious partners are warm and attentive. |
| "Your style is set in childhood forever." | Long-term data shows anxiety and avoidance tend to decline across adulthood. |
| "You need a secure partner to heal." | A steady partner helps, but friends, therapy and your own practice matter too. |
| "Two insecure people can't make it work." | Many do, especially when both understand the pattern and own their part. |

## How to use this in everyday life

Understanding your pattern is the first half. The second half is doing one small thing differently the next time the alarm goes off.

### If you lean anxious

- **Catch the alarm early.** Notice the physical signs: tight chest, checking your phone, rereading messages. Name it: "My alarm is going off."
- **Calm your body first.** Slow breathing with a long exhale, a short walk or moving to another room. You'll think more clearly once your heart rate drops.
- **Ask for what you need directly.** One clear message works better than three hinting ones.
- **Separate facts from stories.** Our guide to [overthinking in a relationship](/blog/how-to-stop-overthinking-in-a-relationship/) has a step-by-step way to do this.

Try: "I'm feeling a bit unsure today. Could you tell me we're okay?"

### If you lean avoidant

- **Take the space, but announce it.** The problem is rarely the break. It's the silence around it. Say that you need time, and say when you'll be back.
- **Come back when you said you would.** That's what builds trust. Each time you return, your partner's alarm learns it can relax.
- **Practise one small vulnerable sentence a week.** It doesn't have to be a big speech. "I missed you today" counts.

Try: "I need an hour on my own to think. Can we talk at 8?"

### If you're secure

Stay patient, but don't become the full-time therapist in the relationship. Your steadiness can help a partner who leans anxious or avoidant, and you still get to have needs and limits.

Try: "I'm not going anywhere. And I also need us to talk about this calmly."

### If you're fearful-avoidant

Go slowly. Notice the push and pull without judging yourself for it. It helps to tell a partner about the pattern on a calm day, so it doesn't come as a shock on a hard one. Support from a therapist trained in attachment work is especially worthwhile here.

Try: "I want to be close, and it also scares me a bit. Can we go slowly?"

### As a couple

Pick a calm moment and each answer three questions: When I feel disconnected, what do I usually do? What am I afraid of in that moment? What would help me feel safe? Then agree on one small ritual for the next time the loop starts, like a code word, a 20-minute break with a set return time, or a hand on the shoulder before the talk begins.

## Quick answers

### Can your attachment style change?

Yes. A study that combined data on 628 people across a 59-year span found that attachment anxiety and avoidance both tended to decrease across adulthood, and people in close relationships scored lower on both. Therapy, reliable relationships and practising new responses all help.

### What is the most common attachment style?

Secure. In Hazan and Shaver's original 1987 study, about 56% of adults described themselves as secure, and secure attachment usually comes out as the most common style in later research too.

### How do I know my partner's attachment style?

You can notice patterns, like how they react to distance or conflict, but it's better not to label them. Talk about what each of you needs when you feel stressed. That conversation is more useful than any quiz result.

### Is an anxious and avoidant relationship doomed?

No. It's a common pairing and it can be hard, because each person's way of coping triggers the other. Couples who learn to spot the pursue and withdraw loop, and who agree on how to take breaks and come back, often do much better.

### Is fearful-avoidant the same as disorganized attachment?

They're closely related. "Disorganized" is the term used in research on children. In adults, the high-anxiety, high-avoidance pattern is usually called fearful or fearful-avoidant.

### What attachment style is the most difficult?

Fearful-avoidant attachment is often described as the hardest to live with, because the person wants closeness and fears it at the same time. It's also a pattern that can improve a lot with steady relationships and support from a therapist.

### Can two avoidant people be in a relationship?

Yes. They may get along easily at first because neither pushes for closeness, but problems can stay hidden for a long time. It helps to agree on regular check-ins, so important feelings get said before they turn into distance.
