When psychologist Mark Leary and his colleagues asked 164 people about a time their feelings were hurt, one pattern stood out. What hurt most wasn't the words themselves. It was what the words seemed to say about the relationship: that the person mattered less than they had thought. Leary called this *relational devaluation*.

That's useful to know in the moment your partner says, "That really hurt me." Your instinct is to answer the question "Was I wrong?" Their real question is usually different: "Do I still matter to you?" This guide walks you through the first five minutes, step by step, with words you can use, including what to say when you honestly didn't mean it.

> **The short answer:**
> When your partner says you hurt their feelings, don't explain yet. Stop what you're doing, repeat back what you heard, acknowledge how it landed, ask what hurt most, and tell them they matter to you. Only after they feel understood should you share what you meant, and then apologize for the impact even if the intent was different.

## Why the first minute matters more than the apology

Three findings explain why the start of this conversation carries so much weight.

**Hurt is about meaning, not just words.** In Leary's 1998 study, hurt feelings were triggered by events that suggested relational devaluation, and the distress was strongly tied to feeling rejected. People were usually hurt by someone they knew well. So a small comment from you can land harder than the same words from a stranger.

**What they think you meant shapes how far they pull away.** Communication researchers Anita Vangelisti and S. L. Young found that when people saw a hurtful comment as intentional, they reported more distance, less satisfaction and less closeness in the relationship than when they saw it as accidental. Feeling disregarded by the person who hurt them, and seeing the comment as part of a pattern, also went with more distancing. In other words, your partner is quietly deciding what your words meant. How you respond is part of the evidence.

**Feeling understood protects the relationship.** In a set of studies by Amie Gordon and Serena Chen, conflict only lowered relationship satisfaction when people felt their partner didn't understand them. When they felt understood, the conflict didn't do the same damage, and in a lab discussion people who felt understood even left more satisfied than they arrived. A 2023 set of five studies with 842 couples by Eri Sasaki, Nickola Overall, Harry Reis and colleagues adds another piece: destructive behavior during hard conversations was highest when both partners felt unloved, and one partner feeling loved could buffer the other's worst reactions.

Put simply: before your partner can hear your side, they need to feel heard and valued. Skipping that step is how a small hurt becomes a long fight.

My take: the hardest part is not explaining yourself straight away. Your intentions matter, but they land much better after your partner feels heard, not before.

## The first five minutes, step by step

### 1. Stop and turn toward them

Put the phone down, turn your body, look at them. If you're in the middle of something, say so and come back fast.

**Say:** "Okay. I'm listening."

**Why:** your attention is the first answer to "Do I matter to you?" Half-listening while you finish a task tells them the opposite.

### 2. Say back what you heard

Repeat the core of what they said, in plain words, without adding your view. This is not agreeing. It's showing you got it.

**Say:** "So when I joked about your cooking in front of my brother, it felt like I was making fun of you."

**Why:** it's the simplest way to make someone feel understood, and it catches misunderstandings early. If you got it wrong, they'll tell you, and you can try again.

### 3. Acknowledge the impact

Name how it landed for them. You don't need to agree with every detail to accept that it hurt.

**Say:** "That makes sense. I can see why that would sting, especially in front of family."

**Why:** this is the antidote the Gottman Institute recommends for defensiveness: take responsibility for your part, even if it's only part of the problem. It turns the conversation from a trial into a shared problem.

### 4. Ask what hurt most

There's often a deeper layer: not the joke itself, but that it happened again, or in front of someone they want to impress.

**Say:** "What was the worst part of it for you?"

**Why:** it shows you care about their experience, not just closing the topic. And the answer tells you what the apology needs to cover.

### 5. Tell them they matter

Answer the question underneath. Keep it short and specific.

**Say:** "You matter to me more than a laugh at dinner. I don't want you to feel small around my family."

**Why:** if hurt is about feeling devalued, the most direct repair is to say clearly that they are valued.

### 6. Then, and only then, share your intent and apologize

Now you can explain what you meant, briefly, and own the impact anyway.

**Say:** "I honestly meant it as teasing, not as a dig. But it hurt you, and I'm sorry. Next time I'll keep that kind of joke between us."

**Why:** intent matters to your partner, as the research above shows. But said first, it sounds like a defense. Said after they feel understood, it sounds like reassurance. For the full structure of a good apology, see [how to apologize to your partner](/blog/how-to-apologize-to-your-partner/).

## Impact first, intent second

Most of us lead with intent because it's what we know best about the situation. Your partner leads with impact, because that's what they lived. The order you say things in changes how they land.

| What you want to say first | What to say first instead |
|---|---|
| "I didn't mean it like that." | "I can see it really hurt you." |
| "You're being too sensitive." | "Tell me more about how it felt." |
| "I was just joking." | "It wasn't funny to you, and that matters." |
| "You do the same thing to me." | "Let's talk about mine first." |
| "I said sorry, what more do you want?" | "What would help right now?" |

You still get to say the things in the left column, or the honest version of them. Just later, and gentler.

## What to say in common situations

### When you really didn't know it would hurt

"I had no idea that would land that way, and I'm glad you told me. Can you help me understand what it touched?" Not knowing isn't a crime. Treating their reaction as unreasonable is what turns it into one.

### When you think they misread you

"I want to understand how you heard it first. Then can I tell you what I was trying to say?" Asking permission to give your side keeps it from sounding like a correction. If they still see it differently afterward, you don't have to agree on what happened to agree that it hurt.

### When it's an old wound

"I know this isn't the first time. I'm sorry I keep landing on that spot." Vangelisti and Young found that hurt that feels like part of a pattern goes with more distancing. Naming the pattern yourself shows you see it too.

### When they tell you by text

"Thank you for telling me. I'm sorry it hurt. Can we talk about it properly tonight?" Then have the conversation by voice. Our list of [what to text after a fight](/blog/what-to-text-after-a-fight/) has more wording for the in-between hours.

### When you're hurt too

"I want to hear you fully. Later, I'd also like to tell you what was going on for me." Put your hurt in the queue, not in the way. One person at a time is how both of you end up feeling heard.

> **What most advice gets wrong**
> A lot of advice says intent doesn't matter, only impact does. The research doesn't quite say that. People pull away more when they believe a hurt was deliberate, so your intent genuinely matters to your partner. The problem is timing. Lead with intent and it sounds like "your feelings are wrong". Lead with understanding, and the same explanation becomes good news: "I didn't mean to hurt you, and I still care that I did."

## What not to say

- **"I'm sorry you feel that way."** It apologizes for their feelings, not your action.
- **"You know I'd never hurt you on purpose."** It asks them to comfort you in the middle of their hurt.
- **"Can we not do this right now?"** If you really can't, name a time: "I want to hear this properly. Can we talk at eight?"
- **A long explanation before any acknowledgment.** Even a good reason sounds like an excuse if it comes first.
- **Silence.** Saying nothing to "avoid making it worse" usually reads as not caring.

> **Example: the comment at the party**
> On the drive home from a friend's birthday, Leah told Omar that his comment about her "always running late" in front of everyone had embarrassed her. His first thought was that he'd said it with a smile, and that she was in fact late. He didn't say that. He said, "So it felt like I was making fun of you in front of our friends." She nodded. He asked what the worst part was. "That they laughed, and you joined in." He told her she mattered more to him than the joke, and only then said he'd meant it lightly, but he could see it didn't land lightly. The whole conversation took four minutes. They stopped for ice cream on the way home.

## Quick answers

### What should I say when my partner says I hurt their feelings?

Start with something like "Okay, I'm listening," then repeat back what you heard and acknowledge that it hurt. Save your explanation for after they feel understood, and then apologize for the impact.

### Should I apologize if I didn't mean to hurt them?

Yes. You can apologize for the impact while being honest that it wasn't your intent: "I didn't mean it that way, but it hurt you, and I'm sorry." Both things can be true.

### What if I think my partner is overreacting?

Listen first anyway. Ask what the comment touched for them. Often there's a reason it hit harder than you expected, like an old wound or a pattern. If you still disagree afterward, you can say so calmly without dismissing how they felt.

### How do I stop getting defensive when my partner is hurt?

Notice the urge to explain and give yourself one job instead: make them feel understood. Breathing slowly, sitting down and repeating back what you heard all help. Defensiveness usually fades once you stop treating the conversation as a verdict on you. ## A note on safety

This guide is for ordinary hurts in a relationship where both people feel safe. If you're being blamed for everything, told you're "hurting" your partner whenever you have a need of your own, or you feel afraid, that's a different situation. Please talk to someone you trust or contact a local domestic abuse helpline or support service. Our guide to [signs of a toxic relationship](/blog/signs-of-a-toxic-relationship/) may help.

In the US, you can reach the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, by texting START to 88788 or at [thehotline.org](https://www.thehotline.org/). Outside the US, contact a local helpline.
