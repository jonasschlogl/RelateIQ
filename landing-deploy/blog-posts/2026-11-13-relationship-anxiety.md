Everything is fine. They texted good morning, you had a nice weekend, nothing is wrong. And yet there's this voice: what if they're losing interest? What if this is too good to last? What if they leave?

That's relationship anxiety. It can show up in new relationships and long ones, and it often has very little to do with how your partner actually feels. The good news is that it can be understood and calmed. This guide covers the seven most common signs, where relationship anxiety comes from, what research says about worry and attachment, and practical steps that help, both in the moment and over time.

## What is relationship anxiety?

Relationship anxiety is ongoing worry, doubt or insecurity about your relationship, even when there's no clear reason for it. It often looks like needing reassurance, fearing rejection or questioning whether your partner really cares.

It's closely linked to an anxious [attachment style](/blog/how-attachment-styles-work-in-relationships/), where closeness feels important but uncertain, so your mind keeps scanning for signs of danger. In Hazan and Shaver's classic 1987 study, about one in five adults described their romantic relationships in a way that matched anxious attachment. So if this sounds like you, you're far from alone.

> **Why it works this way**
> Think of relationship anxiety as a very sensitive alarm. Its job is to protect your connection, so it goes off at the smallest sign that something might be wrong: a short text, a tired face, a slow reply. The alarm isn't lying on purpose. It's just tuned to catch every possible threat, which means it also catches a lot of things that aren't threats at all. The goal isn't to rip the alarm out. It's to learn to check whether there's actually a fire before you react.

## 7 signs of relationship anxiety

### 1. You need a lot of reassurance

You ask "are we okay?" or "do you still love me?" often, and the relief doesn't last long.

### 2. A slow reply sends you spiralling

Twenty minutes without a text and your mind has already written a whole story about why.

### 3. You read into small things

A shorter message, a different tone or a tired face feels like proof that something is wrong.

### 4. You hide your needs to keep the peace

You stay quiet about what bothers you because you're afraid speaking up will push them away.

### 5. You test your partner

You pull back to see if they'll chase you, or bring up their ex to see how they react.

### 6. You can't enjoy the good moments

Even when things are going well, part of you is waiting for it to end.

### 7. You worry about whether it's the "right" relationship

Doubts run on repeat, not because of real problems, but because certainty feels impossible.

> **Example: the reassurance loop**
> Eva asks her boyfriend if he's happy with her. He says yes, and she feels better for about an hour. Then she notices he seems quiet at dinner and asks again, in a different way: "You'd tell me if something was wrong, right?" He says of course. By the end of the week she's asked some version of the question a dozen times, and he's started to feel frustrated, which she reads as proof that something is wrong.
>
> The reassurance wasn't the problem. Needing it again and again, because the anxiety never really settled, was.

## Where it comes from

Relationship anxiety usually has roots, not reasons. Past breakups, being cheated on, growing up with unpredictable care, or simply a more anxious temperament can all play a part.

Fear of being alone can also make it worse. In research led by Stephanie Spielmann, people with a stronger fear of being single were more willing to settle for less responsive partners and to stay in relationships they weren't happy in. That fear affected men and women alike. In other words, anxiety doesn't only make good relationships feel shaky. It can also keep people in the wrong ones.

## Your worries are usually worse than reality

In a 2020 study, psychologists Lucas LaFreniere and Michelle Newman asked people with generalized anxiety disorder to write down their worries over ten days, and then track what actually happened. 91.4% of the worries didn't come true, and of the ones that did, about 30% turned out better than expected.

It was a small study, and relationship worries aren't exactly the same. But the lesson fits: a worry can feel completely certain and still be wrong. The strength of the feeling tells you how anxious you are, not how likely the fear is.

## How to calm relationship anxiety

### Name the feeling, not the story

Separating the feeling from the story gives you a bit of distance. It sounds small, but it changes how the thought feels: from a fact to something your mind is doing.

Instead of: "They're losing interest."

Try: "I'm feeling anxious right now."

### Pause before you act

When the urge to send five messages or check their profile hits, wait ten minutes. Anxiety peaks and then drops, if you let it.

A simple way to use those ten minutes is cyclic sighing. In a 2023 Stanford study, 111 volunteers who practised breathing exercises for five minutes a day for a month improved their mood more than a mindfulness group, and cyclic sighing helped most. Breathe in through your nose, take a second short sip of air at the top, then breathe out slowly through your mouth. Repeat for a few minutes.

> **Why it works**
> Every time you act on the anxiety (sending the extra text, checking their profile), you get quick relief, and your brain learns that the anxiety was right to raise the alarm. Every time you pause and the feeling passes on its own, your brain learns the opposite: that uncertainty is survivable. Pausing feels harder at first, but it's what makes the alarm quieter over time.

### Share it calmly, once

You can tell your partner what's going on without asking them to fix it. Clear, kind requests work far better than repeated questions. If you want to check how a message sounds before you send it, that's exactly what tools like Message check are for.

Try: "Sometimes I get anxious when I don't hear from you. It's not about you doing something wrong. It would help if you texted when you're running late."

### Look at the evidence

Write down what your partner actually does that shows care. Anxiety tends to ignore the evidence and focus on the gaps. A list helps balance it. Our guide to [overthinking in a relationship](/blog/how-to-stop-overthinking-in-a-relationship/) has a simple "facts versus story" exercise for this.

### Give worry a set time

Instead of fighting every worry as it appears, set aside 15 to 30 minutes in the evening as "worry time". When an anxious thought shows up during the day, write it down and save it for then. Many people find that by the evening, most worries have lost their power.

### Build a life outside the relationship

Friends, hobbies and your own goals make the relationship feel less like the only thing holding you up. That reduces the pressure on both of you.

### Get support if it's taking over

If anxiety is affecting your sleep, work or daily life, a therapist can help. Attachment patterns can change, especially with support. A study that combined data on 628 people across a 59-year span found that attachment anxiety tends to rise in young adulthood and then decline steadily through life, and people in close relationships scored lower on it at every age.

## In the moment: a quick plan

| When this happens | Try this |
|---|---|
| No reply for a while | Name the feeling, breathe for 2 minutes, list 3 other explanations |
| Urge to ask "are we okay?" again | Wait 10 minutes. If it's still important, ask once |
| You read a text as cold | Read it as if a friend sent it to you |
| You want to test them | Ask for what you actually need instead |
| A good moment feels scary | Notice it, and let yourself enjoy it anyway |

## Is it anxiety, or is something really wrong?

Sometimes your gut is picking up on something real. If your partner is inconsistent, hides things or regularly makes you feel unwanted, the problem may not be your anxiety. Our guides on [breadcrumbing](/blog/breadcrumbing/) and [emotionally unavailable partners](/blog/emotionally-unavailable-partner/) can help you tell the difference.

A useful question is: do I feel this way with this person specifically, or in every relationship? Another is: can I describe a pattern of their behaviour in facts, without interpretation? If yes, that pattern deserves a calm, direct conversation.

## Quick answers

### Is relationship anxiety normal?

Some worry is very common, especially early on or after a hurt. It becomes a problem when it's constant, drives you to seek reassurance again and again, or stops you from enjoying the relationship.

### Can relationship anxiety ruin a relationship?

It can put strain on it if it leads to constant testing, checking or accusations. But it's very workable, especially when you name it, share it calmly and practise pausing before you act on it.

### How do I stop needing reassurance all the time?

Notice the urge, pause, and calm your body before asking. Then ask once, clearly, instead of many times. Over time, sitting with a little uncertainty makes the anxiety smaller.

### Is relationship anxiety a sign I'm with the wrong person?

Not necessarily. Anxiety can show up in good relationships, often because of past experiences. The difference is whether there's a real pattern of behaviour you can describe in facts, or mostly a feeling.

### When should I see a therapist for relationship anxiety?

If it's affecting your sleep, work or mood most days, or if it comes with panic, a therapist can help a lot. Cognitive behavioural therapy has good evidence for anxiety.
