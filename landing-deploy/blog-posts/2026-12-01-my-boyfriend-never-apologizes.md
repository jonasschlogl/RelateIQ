He forgot the dinner with your parents. Or he snapped at you in front of friends. You wait for the "sorry". Instead you get "Well, you didn't remind me", or a change of subject, or a peace offering two days later (coffee in bed, a joke, acting like nothing happened) without a word about what went wrong.

If "my boyfriend never apologizes" is something you've typed more than once, you're probably tired of being the one who says sorry first, even when it wasn't your fault. This guide explains why some people find it so hard to apologize, how to tell which kind of "never sorry" you're dealing with, and what to say that has a real chance of changing it.

> **The short answer:**
> Most people who rarely apologize aren't heartless. Apologizing feels like a threat to how they see themselves, they underestimate how much it would help, or they genuinely don't see the problem the way you do. You'll get further by asking for one specific acknowledgment ("I need to hear that you get why that hurt") than by demanding the word "sorry". If he refuses to ever take responsibility and blames you for everything, that's a different problem, and it's worth taking seriously.

## Which kind of "never sorry" is it?

Start by figuring out which one sounds like him. They look similar from the outside, but they need different responses.

| What it looks like | What's often going on | What tends to help |
|---|---|---|
| He makes up for it (fixes the thing, gets extra sweet) but never says the words | Saying sorry feels exposing, so he apologizes through actions | Tell him the actions count, and that you also need one sentence |
| He explains, justifies or turns it around ("You were late too") | Apologizing feels like admitting he's a bad person | Separate the act from who he is, and lower the stakes |
| He doesn't think he did anything wrong | He sees the situation differently, or rates it as less serious | Explain the impact, not the rule he broke |
| He never takes responsibility for anything and you end up apologizing for his behavior | A pattern of blame and control | Look at the bigger pattern, not the apology (see the safety section) |

Most people recognize the first three, often a mix of them. The fourth is rarer, and it's the one where a better conversation won't be enough on its own.

## Why some people find "sorry" so hard

**Not apologizing can feel good, at least in the moment.** In studies by Tyler Okimoto, Michael Wenzel and Kyli Hedrick, people who refused to apologize felt more powerful, more in control and truer to themselves than people who did nothing either way. The researchers didn't measure how the other person felt, which is the catch: the refuser's short-term boost comes at the relationship's expense.

**Apologizing first feels risky.** Research by Shereen Chaudhry and Valeria Burdea found that when both people share some of the blame, people are more willing to apologize first if they expect an apology back. Apologizing into silence feels like losing. That's why so many couples get stuck in a standoff where both are waiting.

**People overestimate how bad it will feel.** Apology researcher Karina Schumann points out that people tend to overestimate the costs of apologizing and underestimate the benefits, and that once they do apologize, both people usually feel better. She also notes that in her research with couples, people apologized more when they felt more empathy for their partner.

**Some men simply notice fewer offenses.** In a well-known pair of studies, Schumann and Michael Ross found that men apologized less often than women mainly because they rated the same offenses as less serious. When men thought they'd done something wrong, they apologized about as often as women did. So "he never apologizes" can sometimes mean "he genuinely didn't register it as a big deal".

My take: I find that last finding oddly hopeful. If part of the problem is that he doesn't see the impact, then explaining the impact is something you can actually do. You can't make someone feel guilty, but you can help them see.

## What a new 2026 study adds

A study published in November 2026 in *The Journal of Social Psychology* by Brianna Davison, C. Ward Struthers, Jingyuan Sophie Li and Amy Muise looked at what happens to the hurt partner when an apology doesn't come. Across three studies with more than 1,100 people, a partner who seemed unapologetic went with more destructive reactions afterwards: holding a grudge, pulling away, withholding forgiveness.

The effect was stronger for people with high attachment anxiety, those who already worry about whether their partner really cares. And in their experiment, a defensive non-apology ("I didn't do anything wrong") made people react worse than no response at all.

The effects were modest, and most reactions were fairly mild. But it explains something many people feel: the missing apology isn't really about manners. It's about whether you can trust that your hurt matters to him. If you notice yourself holding on to things longer than you'd like, that's a normal response, not a character flaw. Our guide on [relationship anxiety](/blog/relationship-anxiety/) has ideas for the anxious side of this.

## What to say instead of "you never apologize"

"You never apologize" is usually true, and it almost never works. It's a verdict about his character, and people defend their character. Try narrowing it down to one moment and one need.

1. **Pick one incident.** "On Saturday, when you laughed about my job in front of Dan and Priya."
2. **Describe the impact.** "I felt embarrassed, and like you weren't on my side."
3. **Ask for acknowledgment, not groveling.** "I don't need a big speech. I need to hear that you get why it hurt."
4. **Lower the stakes for him.** "It doesn't make you a bad boyfriend. It was one moment, and I want us to be able to clear these up."
5. **Notice when he tries.** If he says "Yeah, that wasn't cool", take it, even if it's not the perfect apology you imagined.

That last step matters more than it looks. If every attempt gets critiqued ("That's not a real apology"), he learns that trying is risky, and he stops.

My take: I'd stop waiting for the word "sorry" and start listening for ownership. "I messed up" or "You're right, I shouldn't have said that" is an apology, even if it doesn't sound like the one in your head. What you're really asking for is to know he sees you.

> **Example: the forgotten dinner**
> Nora's boyfriend Alex missed her birthday dinner with her family because he "lost track of time" at the gym. The next day he brought her favorite pastries and acted normal. Nora felt the old resentment rising. Instead of "You never say sorry for anything," she said: "The pastries are sweet, and I can tell you feel bad. But I need you to actually say it. My family waited forty minutes, and I felt like I didn't matter." Alex went quiet, then said, "You're right. I screwed up, and I'm sorry you had to sit there like that." It was the first time in months he'd said the word.
>

## If you're the one who always apologizes

Many people in this situation start apologizing for things that aren't theirs, just to end the tension. It works in the short term and builds resentment in the long term. It also teaches both of you that your hurt gets resolved by your apology, not his.

You can stop that without a big announcement. Next time, instead of "Sorry, I overreacted", try "I'm not going to apologize for being upset, but I do want to talk about it when we're both calm." ## A note on safety

An occasional stubborn streak is common. But if your partner never takes responsibility, regularly tells you that you're too sensitive or remembering things wrong, and leaves you apologizing for his behavior, that pattern deserves a closer look.
