When a relationship feels broken, the first instinct is to fix everything at once. Have the big talk, settle every old argument, change how you both behave. It almost always backfires. What works better is smaller: a few calm steps, taken in a sensible order.

## Step 1: Decide what "better" looks like

"Fixing it" is too vague to act on. Before you talk to your partner, get clear on what would feel different if things were better. More affection? A fairer split at home? Feeling listened to? Write it down in a sentence or two. It keeps you from arguing about everything and helps you ask for something specific.

## Step 2: Stop the escalation first

Nothing gets rebuilt while you're still hurting each other. The Gottman Method describes four habits that predict trouble when they become routine, often called the Four Horsemen:

- **Criticism**: attacking the person instead of the problem.
- **Contempt**: sarcasm, eye-rolling, mockery.
- **Defensiveness**: counter-complaints and playing the victim.
- **Stonewalling**: shutting down and going silent.

You don't have to be perfect. Just notice which one you slip into most, and try its antidote. A softer start-up for criticism, appreciation for contempt, taking some responsibility for defensiveness, and a short break (with a promise to come back) for stonewalling.

## Step 3: Have one small conversation about one thing

Skip the "state of the relationship" talk. Pick one topic and a calm moment, not right after work and not in the middle of a fight. Then say how you'd like it to go: "I'd like to talk about how we split the housework. I'm not blaming you, I'd just like us to find something that feels fair."

## Step 4: Rebuild the good moments

Closeness rarely comes back through one big conversation. It comes back through lots of small moments. Gottman's research found that stable couples keep about five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict, and far more in everyday life.

So notice what your partner does right and say thank you. Make a little time that has nothing to do with problems: a walk, a meal, ten minutes of real conversation.

## Step 5: Ask what each of you needs

Instead of "What's wrong with us?", try "What would help you feel closer to me right now?" Then answer the same question yourself. It turns blame into information, which is much easier to work with.

## Step 6: Get help when you're stuck

If you've tried this for a few weeks and the same fights keep coming back, or one of you has stopped trying, a couples therapist can spot patterns that are hard to see from the inside. An AI coach can be useful for thinking things through and rehearsing difficult conversations in between sessions, but it doesn't replace professional care.

## A note on safety

All of this assumes two people who feel safe with each other. If you're afraid of your partner, feel controlled, or are being hurt, fixing the relationship isn't the goal. Please contact a local support service or helpline.
