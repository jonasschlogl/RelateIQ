When a relationship feels broken, the instinct is to fix everything at once: have *the* big talk, settle every old argument, change how you both behave. That usually backfires. What helps is the opposite — a few small, calm steps, taken in the right order.

## Step 1: Decide what "better" looks like

"Fixing" is vague. Before you talk to your partner, get clear for yourself: what would feel different if things were better? More affection? More fairness at home? Feeling listened to? Write it down in one or two sentences. This stops you from arguing about everything and helps you ask for something specific.

## Step 2: Stop the escalation first

You can't rebuild anything while you're still hurting each other. The Gottman Method describes four patterns that predict trouble when they become habits, sometimes called the Four Horsemen: **criticism** (attacking the person, not the problem), **contempt** (sarcasm, eye-rolling, mockery), **defensiveness** (counter-complaints, playing the victim) and **stonewalling** (shutting down and going silent).

You don't need to be perfect. Just notice which one you slip into most, and try the antidote: a softer start for criticism, appreciation for contempt, taking responsibility for defensiveness, and a short break (with a promise to return) for stonewalling.

## Step 3: Have one small conversation about one thing

Resist the "state of the relationship" talk. Choose one topic, pick a calm time (not right after work, not in the middle of a fight) and say how you'd like it to go: "I'd like to talk about how we split the housework. I'm not blaming you — I'd like us to find something that feels fair."

## Step 4: Rebuild the good moments

Connection rarely returns through one big conversation. It returns through many small moments. Gottman's research suggests that happy couples keep roughly five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict, and many more in everyday life. Notice what your partner does right, say thank you, and make a little time that has nothing to do with problems: a walk, a meal, ten minutes of real conversation.

## Step 5: Ask what each of you needs

Instead of "What's wrong with us?", try "What would help you feel closer to me right now?" — and answer the same question yourself. This turns blame into information.

## Step 6: Get help when you're stuck

If you've tried this for a few weeks and the same fights keep returning, or one of you has stopped trying, a couples therapist can help you see patterns that are hard to spot from the inside. An AI coach can be useful for thinking things through and rehearsing difficult conversations in between — but it doesn't replace professional care.

## A note on safety

Everything above assumes two people who feel safe with each other. If you are afraid of your partner, feel controlled, or are being hurt, "fixing the relationship" is not the goal. Please contact a local support service or helpline.
