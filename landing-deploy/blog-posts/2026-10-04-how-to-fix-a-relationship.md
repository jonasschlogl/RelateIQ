When a relationship feels broken, the first instinct is to fix everything at once. Have the big talk, settle every old argument, change how you both behave. It almost always backfires. You end up arguing about the whole relationship instead of one thing you could actually change, and both of you walk away more hopeless than before.

What works better is smaller: a few calm steps, taken in a sensible order. This guide is built on decades of couples research, mostly from John Gottman and his colleagues, who filmed and measured couples for years to see what separates relationships that recover from ones that don't. You'll find the six steps, why each one works, and what it sounds like in real life.

## First: is this a relationship that can be fixed?

Most relationships that feel broken are stuck, not finished. Distance, constant arguments, feeling like flatmates, losing the spark: these are common, and they often respond to changes in how you talk and how you spend time together.

There are two important exceptions. If you're afraid of your partner, feel controlled, or are being hurt, fixing the relationship isn't the goal. Your safety is. And if one of you has already decided to leave, no technique can replace that decision. If you're not sure whether you want to stay, our guide to [deciding whether to break up](/blog/should-i-break-up-with-my-partner/) can help you think it through first.

| Usually fixable | Needs more than these steps |
|---|---|
| Constant arguing about everyday things | Fear, threats, control or violence |
| Feeling distant or like flatmates | One partner has clearly checked out |
| The spark has faded | Ongoing lying or cheating that continues |
| Stress has pushed you apart | Addiction that isn't being treated |

## Step 1: Decide what "better" looks like

"Fixing it" is too vague to act on. Before you talk to your partner, get clear on what would feel different if things were better. More affection? A fairer split at home? Feeling listened to? Write it down in a sentence or two. It keeps you from arguing about everything and helps you ask for something specific.

> **Example: from "everything's wrong" to one sentence**
> Klara feels her relationship with Ondřej has become cold. When she sits down to write what "better" would look like, she realises it's mostly two things: she wants him to ask about her day, and she wants one evening a week without screens. That's something they can actually talk about, unlike "you don't love me like before".

## Step 2: Stop the escalation first

Nothing gets rebuilt while you're still hurting each other. Gottman's research identified four habits that reliably predict relationship breakdown when they become routine. He called them the Four Horsemen:

| Horseman | What it looks like | Antidote |
|---|---|---|
| Criticism | Attacking the person instead of the problem | A soft start: a feeling and a request |
| Contempt | Sarcasm, eye-rolling, mockery | Appreciation and respect, said out loud |
| Defensiveness | Counter-complaints, playing the victim | Taking responsibility for your part |
| Stonewalling | Shutting down and going silent | A short break, with a promise to come back |

You don't have to be perfect. Just notice which one you slip into most, and practise its antidote. The Gottman Institute describes contempt as the single biggest predictor of divorce, so if eye-rolling and mocking have crept in, start there.

> **Why it works**
> Each horseman pushes the other person into self-defence, and once someone is defending themselves, they can't listen. That's why couples can argue about the same thing for years without getting anywhere. Removing the horsemen doesn't solve the problem by itself. It creates the conditions in which a problem can be solved.

## Step 3: Have one small conversation about one thing

Skip the "state of the relationship" talk. Pick one topic and a calm moment, not right after work and not in the middle of a fight. How you open matters more than almost anything else: in Gottman's research, the first three minutes of a conversation predicted how it would end 96% of the time.

Try: "I'd like to talk about how we split the housework. I'm not blaming you, I'd just like us to find something that feels fair."

Keep it to one topic, end with one specific agreement, and stop while it's still going well. A short conversation that ends with a small plan builds far more hope than a long one that ends in tears.

## Step 4: Rebuild the good moments

Closeness rarely comes back through one big conversation. It comes back through lots of small moments. Gottman's research found that stable couples keep about five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict, and using this kind of data, he and Robert Levenson predicted which couples would divorce with over 90% accuracy.

Positive moments don't have to be big. A thank-you for something ordinary, a laugh, a hand on the shoulder, a real question about their day. In a study that followed newlyweds for six years, couples who stayed together had responded warmly to each other's small bids for attention 86% of the time, compared with 33% for couples who later divorced.

A practical way to start:

- **Say one specific thank-you a day.** "Thanks for sorting the car, that took a weight off me."
- **Take ten minutes each evening to really talk.** Not about logistics. About how the day actually felt.
- **Plan one thing a week that has nothing to do with problems.** A walk, a meal, a film. Our [date night questions](/blog/date-night-questions-for-couples/) can help it feel like a date again.

## Step 5: Ask what each of you needs

Instead of "What's wrong with us?", try a question that points forward. Then answer the same question yourself. It turns blame into information, which is much easier to work with.

Try: "What would help you feel closer to me right now?"

> **Example: two different answers**
> When Martin and Eva ask each other this, Martin says he'd love more physical affection. Eva says she'd feel closer if he took over the morning routine with the kids, so she isn't exhausted by 9 am. Neither answer is what the other expected. Both are easy to act on.

## Step 6: Get help when you're stuck

If you've tried this for a few weeks and the same fights keep coming back, or one of you has stopped trying, a couples therapist can spot patterns that are hard to see from the inside. It's usually easier to change a pattern before years of hurt have built up around it, so it's worth considering sooner rather than later.

An AI coach like RelationshipAI can be useful for thinking things through in Coach Chat and rehearsing difficult conversations in Partner Practice between sessions, but it doesn't replace professional care.

## What if only one of you is trying?

It's very common for one partner to be more motivated than the other, at least at first. One person can change the tone a lot: a softer start, a quicker apology, more appreciation. Often the other person responds in kind within a few weeks, because positive moments tend to invite more of the same.

But a relationship needs two people in the long run. If you've been trying alone for a long time and nothing is changing, say so clearly and calmly. Their response to that conversation is important information.

Try: "I've been really trying to make things better between us, and I need to know you want that too. What are you willing to do?"

## How long does it take?

There's no fixed timeline. Many couples notice a calmer atmosphere within a few weeks of removing the horsemen and adding positive moments. Deeper issues, like broken trust or years of resentment, take longer. Judge it by direction, not speed: is it slowly getting better, or not at all? ## Quick answers

### Can a broken relationship be fixed?

Often, yes, if both people still want to try and both feel safe. What it takes is less about one big conversation and more about stopping the escalation, rebuilding small good moments and talking about one issue at a time.

### Where do I start if everything feels wrong?

Start by stopping things from getting worse: fewer harsh words, more pauses when either of you is flooded. Then pick one small, specific issue to talk about, not the whole relationship at once.

### Can one person fix a relationship alone?

One person can change the tone a lot, because a calmer start and quicker repair often invite the same from the other person. But a relationship needs two people to change in the long run. If only you are trying for weeks, that's important information.

### Is couples therapy worth it?

It can be especially helpful when the same fights keep coming back or you've stopped being able to talk without hurting each other. A trained therapist can spot patterns that are hard to see from inside the relationship.

### What are the signs a relationship can't be fixed?

Ongoing contempt, one partner who has completely checked out, or a relationship where you feel afraid are serious signs. Fear, threats or control are never something to fix with communication skills. Please reach out to a support service.

## A note on safety

All of this assumes two people who feel safe with each other. If you're afraid of your partner, feel controlled, or are being hurt, fixing the relationship isn't the goal. Please contact a local support service or helpline.
