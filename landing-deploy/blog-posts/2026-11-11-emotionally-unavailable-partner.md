They're fun, kind and great company. But whenever things get a little deeper, they change the subject, go quiet or suddenly get busy. You feel close one day and miles apart the next.

If that sounds familiar, you might be with an emotionally unavailable partner. It's one of the most searched relationship questions for a reason: it's confusing, and it can leave you wondering whether the problem is you.

## What does emotionally unavailable mean?

An emotionally unavailable person finds it hard to share feelings, accept closeness or rely on someone else. They may want a relationship, but they keep part of themselves out of reach.

It's often linked to an avoidant [attachment style](/blog/how-attachment-styles-work-in-relationships/). In a large, nationally representative US study from 1997, about 25% of adults described themselves in a way that matched avoidant attachment. So this isn't rare, and it isn't always a sign that someone doesn't care.

## 7 signs of an emotionally unavailable partner

### 1. They change the subject when things get personal

You ask how they feel about something important, and they make a joke or talk about something else.

### 2. They pull away after closeness

A great weekend is followed by a few days of distance. Closeness seems to make them need space.

### 3. Plans stay vague

The future is always "we'll see". Talking about next summer or meeting each other's friends makes them uneasy.

### 4. They're there for fun, not for hard times

They're great at a party but disappear when you're sad, stressed or need support.

### 5. Conflict ends in silence

Instead of talking a problem through, they shut down, leave the room or wait for it to blow over.

### 6. You know their opinions, not their feelings

You know their favourite films and what they think about politics, but not what scares them or what they need.

### 7. You feel lonely even when you're together

This is often the clearest sign. You're in a relationship, but you still feel on your own.

## What you can actually do

You can't make someone emotionally available. But you can make it easier for them to open up, and you can protect yourself.

**Name what you notice, gently.** Try: "I've noticed that when we get close, you sometimes go quiet afterwards. I'm not angry, I just want to understand." Soft, specific and without blame works far better than "you never open up".

**Make it safe, not urgent.** People with an avoidant style often open up more when they don't feel pressure. Short, low-stakes conversations, like on a walk or in the car, can feel easier than a big sit-down talk.

**Notice effort, not perfection.** If they're trying, even a little, say so. "I really liked it when you told me about your day yesterday" encourages more of it.

**Look after your own needs.** Keep friends, hobbies and support outside the relationship, so you're not depending on one person for everything. And try not to overanalyse every distant moment. Our guide on [how to stop overthinking in a relationship](/blog/how-to-stop-overthinking-in-a-relationship/) can help with that.

**Decide what you need long term.** If they're unwilling to work on it, or the loneliness doesn't change, it's okay to decide this relationship can't give you what you need.

## Is it them, or is it me?

Sometimes it's worth asking the honest question both ways. If you have an anxious attachment style, a partner who simply needs a bit more space can feel "unavailable". That doesn't mean your feelings aren't valid. It means the two of you may have different needs that are worth talking about openly. Our list of [green flags in a relationship](/blog/green-flags-in-a-relationship/) can help you see what a steady connection usually looks like.

## A note on safety

Emotional distance is not the same as emotional abuse. If a partner uses silence to punish you, controls you, or makes you feel afraid, that's a different situation. Please reach out to a local helpline or support service.
