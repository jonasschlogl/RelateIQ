Common passive-aggressive phrases, and what to say instead

AfterThanks for getting back to me. I was starting to worry.

BeforeMust be nice.

AfterI'm a bit jealous, honestly. I wish I had a free weekend too.

BeforeFine.

AfterI'm not okay with it, but I don't want to argue over text. Can we talk later?

The pattern is the same every time: the passive-aggressive version hides a feeling inside a polite sentence. The direct version names the feeling or the request, so the other person does not have to decode it.

Why texts sound more passive-aggressive than you meant

Written messages lose your tone of voice, and readers tend to fill the gap with the worst reading when they already feel tension. In experiments by Justin Kruger and Nicholas Epley, people believed others would read their tone correctly about 78% of the time. In reality, readers got it right about 56% of the time.

Small signals carry a lot. A Binghamton University study found that one-word replies like "Okay." were seen as less sincere when they ended with a period. That is why "Fine." can sound like a door closing.

What to do if you are actually annoyed

Being annoyed is fine. The problem is when it comes out sideways. Try this structure: what happened, how you feel, what you would like. For example: "When plans changed last minute, I felt a bit brushed off. Next time, can you give me a heads-up?" Quick answers

What makes a text passive-aggressive?

A text is passive-aggressive when it hides annoyance or criticism inside a polite or neutral sentence, for example "Thanks for finally replying" or "No worries, I'll do it myself". The other person feels the jab but can't easily respond to it.

Is "K" or "Fine." passive-aggressive?

Often it is read that way, especially in a tense moment, even if you did not mean it. A few more words, or an exclamation mark, usually fixes it.
