Our method

Grounded in real research, not just name-dropped

A lot of AI relationship apps mention “evidence-based frameworks” without saying which ones, or citing anything. Here’s exactly what RelationshipAI’s coaching is built on, and where those ideas actually come from.

The Gottman Method

Used in Coach Chat

Dr. John Gottman’s research identified four communication patterns (criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling) that reliably predict relationship breakdown. The Gottman Institute describes contempt (mockery, eye-rolling, sarcasm, treating a partner as inferior) as “the single greatest predictor of divorce.” RelationshipAI’s Coach Chat is built to help you spot these patterns in your own words before you send them, and replace them with their known antidotes: starting a hard conversation gently, taking responsibility instead of getting defensive, and self-soothing instead of shutting down.

SourceThe Gottman Institute, “The Four Horsemen: Recognizing Criticism, Contempt, Defensiveness, and Stonewalling”

Attachment theory

Used in Partner Practice

Attachment theory started with John Bowlby’s work on why infants who are separated from a caregiver become distressed, and was extended by Mary Ainsworth’s “Strange Situation” studies, which identified consistent patterns (securely attached, anxious, and avoidant) in how children respond to separation and reunion. In the 1980s, researchers Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver proposed that the same underlying system shapes how adults bond in romantic relationships, which is the basis for the “secure / anxious / avoidant / disorganized” language RelationshipAI uses when you describe your partner for Partner Practice. Knowing your pattern doesn’t fix anything by itself, but it makes it a lot easier to recognize why the same disagreement keeps playing out the same way.

SourceR. Chris Fraley (University of Illinois), “A Brief Overview of Adult Attachment Theory and Research”

Nonviolent Communication (NVC)

Used in Coach Chat rewrites

Nonviolent Communication is a framework developed by psychologist Marshall Rosenberg, laid out in his book Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life. It’s built around separating observations from judgments, naming the actual feeling underneath a complaint, and making a specific, actionable request instead of a vague accusation. It’s the main thing Coach Chat is doing when it helps you rewrite “you never listen to me” into something that names the feeling and asks for something concrete. That’s NVC’s structure, not a generic “be nicer” pass.

Honest by design

What this does and doesn’t mean

What we can say

These three frameworks are well-established in couples therapy and have decades of research behind them individually. What we can honestly say is that RelationshipAI’s prompts and coaching logic are deliberately built around them, instead of generic “be more understanding” advice.

What we can’t say

What we can’t honestly say is that talking to an AI about your relationship has itself been clinically studied the way in-person therapy has. It hasn’t been, by us or (as far as we know) by anyone else building AI coaching tools yet.

So what is it?

RelationshipAI is a tool for thinking more clearly between the moments that matter, not a replacement for a licensed therapist, and we’d rather tell you that plainly than let the framework names do more talking than they should.
