How to rebuild trust after a fight: 6 steps that actually help

A fight can leave more behind than hurt feelings. Sometimes something was said that can't be unsaid, a promise was broken or one of you simply stopped feeling safe. Afterwards you might be polite, even affectionate, but a small voice asks: can I rely on this person when it matters?

Trust is rebuilt less by one big conversation and more by a series of small, boring, consistent things. Here are six steps that tend to help, in a sensible order.

1. Let the heat drop first

Nothing gets repaired while you're both flooded. John Gottman's research describes how, in a heated argument, heart rate can climb so high that it becomes very hard to hear anything your partner says. His guidance is to take a break of around twenty minutes, do something calming and then return. Say it kindly and say you're coming back: "I want to sort this out, and I'm too worked up. Can we talk in an hour?"

2. Own your part, without conditions

Whatever the other person did, there's usually something on your side of the street: a sharp word, a tone, walking out mid-sentence. Name that part clearly. "I'm sorry I raised my voice and called you selfish" does more than "I'm sorry if you were hurt." Skip the "but." Our guide to how to apologize to your partner breaks this down step by step.

3. Listen to the impact, don't defend the intent

When your partner explains how it felt, your instinct will be to explain what you meant. Hold that back. "I didn't mean it that way" may be true, but it can sound like "you shouldn't feel that way." Try "That makes sense. Tell me more," and let them finish. Feeling understood is a large part of feeling safe again.

4. Make one small, specific promise, and keep it

Big vows ("I'll never do that again") are easy to say and hard to believe. A small, concrete change is more credible: "Next time I need a break, I'll tell you I'm stepping out and when I'll be back." Then do exactly that, again and again. Trust grows from promises that are kept, especially the small ones nobody is watching.

5. Be predictable for a while

After a rupture, consistency matters more than intensity. Reply when you said you would. Show up when you said you would. If the fight was about secrecy or hiding something, being open without being asked, such as telling your partner where you'll be or sharing the thing you'd normally keep to yourself, speeds up the repair. It can feel exposing, but it shows you're not keeping anything in reserve.

6. Reconnect on purpose

A relationship can't live on apologies alone. Add good moments back in: a walk, a meal, a shared joke, a plan for the weekend. Gottman's research on "turning toward" found that couples who stayed together responded to each other's small attempts at connection far more often than couples who split. Notice the small bids and answer them. This is the "rebuilding the good moments" part of the process we describe in our guide on how to fix a relationship.

How long does it take?

There's no honest timeline. It depends on what happened, how long it's been building and whether the same thing keeps repeating. If the same fight returns every few weeks, the issue probably isn't one blow-up. It might be one of the patterns we cover in relationship problems that keep coming back.

When it's more than a fight

Some breaches go beyond an argument, like infidelity or a deep betrayal of trust. The Gottman Institute describes a three-phase approach for these situations, called Atone, Attune, Attach. In short, the person who broke the trust takes full responsibility without excuses and offers openness, both partners rebuild understanding of each other's inner world through regular, honest conversation, and then they rebuild closeness, including physical intimacy. This kind of repair is hard to do alone, and it's usually best done with the support of a couples therapist.

If you ever feel afraid of your partner, or the fights involve threats, intimidation or control, that's a safety issue, not a communication problem. Please contact a local support service or helpline.
