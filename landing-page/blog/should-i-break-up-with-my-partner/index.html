Should I break up with my partner? 10 honest questions to help you decide

Some days you're sure you want to leave. Other days you remember why you fell for them and can't imagine life without them. You've made mental lists, asked friends, maybe typed "should I break up with my partner" into Google at 2am.

If you feel torn, you're not weak or indecisive. You're facing one of the hardest decisions there is, and you're facing it with incomplete information, mixed feelings and a lot at stake. This guide won't tell you what to do. Nobody outside your relationship can. What it will do is show you what research has found about this decision, walk you through ten questions that cut through the noise, and help you reach a decision you understand and can stand behind.

Feeling torn is normal

Researchers Samantha Joel, Geoff MacDonald and Elizabeth Page-Gould asked people who were actively deciding whether to break up what was pulling them each way. They found 27 different reasons to stay and 23 reasons to leave. The most common reasons to stay were emotional closeness, what they'd already invested and a sense of obligation. The most common reasons to leave were problems with the partner's personality, a breach of trust and the partner pulling away.

The most striking result: about half of the people had strong reasons on both sides at the same time. Feeling torn isn't a sign you're doing it wrong. It's what this decision usually feels like.

Why it works this way

We tend to imagine that a "right" decision would feel clear. But relationships aren't one thing. They're dozens of things at once: the person, the shared life, the history, the future you pictured, the friends, the flat, the routines. Some of those can be wonderful while others are breaking down. Ambivalence isn't confusion. It's an accurate reading of a mixed situation. The goal isn't to make the mixed feelings disappear. It's to work out which parts matter most to you.

What research says predicts a breakup

In 2010, Benjamin Le and his colleagues combined 137 studies covering 37,761 participants over 33 years of research to find out what best predicts whether unmarried relationships end. Some of the results are surprising.

The strongest predictors of staying together weren't personality traits or how similar the two people were. They were things about the relationship itself: how committed people felt, how much they loved their partner, how much they felt the partner had become part of who they are, and how much they depended on the relationship. Satisfaction, having attractive alternatives and how much people had invested also mattered, but more modestly. Personality traits mattered relatively little.

What does that mean for you? Not that you should stay because you depend on someone, or leave because you're not satisfied today. It means the questions that matter most are about the relationship's core: do you still feel committed, do you still love this person, and do you still want your lives to be connected? Those are the questions below.

10 questions to ask yourself

Take your time with these. Write the answers down if it helps. Notice which ones you avoid, because that often says something too.

1. Do I feel safe with them?

This comes first. If you feel afraid of your partner, are being controlled, threatened or hurt, the question isn't about compatibility. It's about your safety. Skip to the safety note at the end.

2. Do I still respect them?

Relationship researcher John Gottman found that contempt, things like eye-rolling, mocking and talking down to each other, was the single biggest predictor of divorce in his studies. Disagreements are normal. Losing respect is much harder to come back from, because respect is what lets you take your partner's view seriously when you disagree.

Ask yourself honestly: when you think about your partner, is the main feeling frustration ("I wish they would...") or disdain ("They're pathetic")? Frustration can be worked with. Disdain is a warning sign.

3. Is this a bad season or a bad relationship?

A new baby, a job loss, illness, a move or caring for a parent can make any relationship feel worse for a while. Ask yourself: was it good before this period? Would it likely be good again when this passes? If the problems started with the stress, they may ease with it. If they were there long before, the stress may only be making them impossible to ignore.

4. Have we actually tried to fix it?

Not "I've hinted for months", but a clear, calm conversation about what's wrong and what you need. Many problems feel unsolvable only because they've never been said out loud. If your partner doesn't know how serious this is, they haven't had a real chance to respond. Our guide on how to fix a relationship is a good starting point.

5. Is the same fight coming back again and again?

According to the Gottman Institute, 69% of relationship conflicts are about perpetual problems: lasting differences in personality, values or needs that may never be fully resolved. So a recurring argument alone doesn't mean you're incompatible. Almost every couple has them.

The real question is whether you can talk about it without it getting worse every time. Can you laugh about it sometimes? Does either of you ever soften? Or does each round leave more damage? Our article on relationship problems that keep coming back explains what usually sits underneath.

6. Do we want the same big things?

Children, where to live, marriage, faith, how to spend money, how much time to spend together. Love matters, but big life goals that really clash rarely disappear on their own. Sometimes one person compromises and is happy with it. Sometimes the compromise turns into resentment. Be honest about which one it would be for you.

7. If nothing changed, would I be okay in five years?

Not the best-case version of your partner, not who they could be if they finally changed. The person they are right now, with the effort they're making right now. Imagine the same relationship five years from now and notice how your body reacts. Relief? Dread? A shrug?

8. Am I staying for love, or mostly out of fear?

Fear of being alone, of starting over, of hurting them or of what people will say are all very human. But they can quietly make the decision for you. In research led by Stephanie Spielmann, people with a stronger fear of being single were more willing to settle for less responsive partners and to stay in relationships they weren't happy with. If fear is the main reason you're staying, it's worth being honest about that.

9. Does my partner make me feel more like myself, or less?

Healthy relationships usually help you grow. You feel more confident, more curious, more yourself. If you feel smaller, quieter or constantly on edge, pay attention to that. Our list of green flags in a relationship can help you compare.

10. What would I tell a friend in my situation?

We're often clearer about other people's lives than our own. Imagine your best friend described your relationship to you, word for word. What would you say to them? Would you be worried? Relieved for them? Would you tell them to try one more time, or ask why they're still there?

Three situations, three different answers

These examples are illustrative, not real people, but they show how the same question ("should I leave?") can have very different answers.

Example: the bad season

Anna and Peter have been arguing constantly since their daughter was born eight months ago. Anna feels unseen and exhausted. But when she thinks back, the first three years were warm and easy, and Peter still makes her laugh on the rare evenings they're alone.

Her answers point to a bad season: respect is intact, the problems started with a huge life change, and they've never had a calm conversation about how the baby changed things. Her next step isn't a breakup. It's a real talk, some practical help and maybe a few sessions with a couples counsellor.

Example: the life-goals clash

Chris is 34 and wants children. His partner of five years has always said she doesn't, and recently said it more firmly. They love each other, rarely fight and have a good life together.

Nothing here is "wrong" with either of them. But question 6 has a clear answer, and question 7 makes Chris feel a quiet dread. Sometimes the hardest breakups are between two good people who want different lives.

Example: the slow fade of respect

Lucia notices she rolls her eyes when her partner talks about his job. He makes sarcastic comments about her friends. They both apologise less than they used to, and evenings feel like living with a flatmate who's slightly annoyed with you.

This is the pattern Gottman warns about most. It isn't automatically the end, but it's serious. If both of them want to try, it needs real work, ideally with a therapist, and soon. If only one of them wants to try, that's an answer too.

What most advice gets wrong

A lot of articles treat breaking up as a test you pass or fail. Count the red flags, add up the score, done. Real life is messier. You can love someone and still not be right together. You can have real problems and still have a relationship worth fighting for.

The goal isn't to find the "right" answer in one night. It's to make a decision you understand and can stand behind, whichever way it goes.

A calmer way to decide

If you've been going back and forth for months, a bit of structure can help.

  1. Write down your answers to the ten questions. Notice which answers come easily and which ones you skip.
  2. Separate the solvable from the unsolvable. Chores, time together and how you argue can often change. Different life goals, broken respect and fear usually don't change by themselves.
  3. Have one clear conversation. Tell your partner how serious this is and what would need to change. Their response is important information.
  4. Give it a defined time. Agree on something concrete for a few weeks or months, and then honestly check in with yourself. Open-ended "let's see" periods tend to drag on.
  5. Get an outside view. A therapist, a counsellor or a trusted friend who isn't involved can notice what you can't.

Why it works

When you're torn, your mind tends to cycle between two pictures: the best moments of the relationship and the worst ones. Writing things down, testing whether change is possible and setting a time frame moves you from that loop into real information. You stop asking "how do I feel today?" and start asking "what actually happened when we tried?" That's a much better basis for a decision you'll have to live with.

If you decide to stay

Make it an active choice, not just avoiding the decision. Tell your partner what needs to change, and agree on something concrete. Consider couples counselling if you've been stuck for a long time. And if trust was broken, our guide on how to rebuild trust after a fight can help.

In one study by psychologist Gary Lewandowski, people who spent 15 to 30 minutes a day for three days writing about the positive sides of their breakup felt better afterwards, especially when the breakup had been mutual. Quick answers

How do I know if it's just a rough patch?

Rough patches usually have a clear cause, like stress, a move or a busy period, and both of you still want to make it work. If the problems were there long before and nothing changes even after honest conversations, it may be more than a rough patch.

Is it normal to doubt a long relationship?

Yes. Almost everyone in a long relationship has moments of doubt, and research on people deciding whether to break up found that about half had strong reasons to stay and to leave at the same time. What matters is how often, how strong, and whether the doubts are about specific problems you can work on.

Should I break up if I'm not in love anymore?

Not necessarily right away. Feelings in long relationships rise and fall, and they're strongly affected by stress and how much time you spend together. But if the loss of love has lasted a long time and comes with indifference or contempt, take it seriously. Love was one of the strongest predictors of staying together in the research.

Should I break up before the holidays?

There's no perfect time. If you've already decided, waiting weeks just to get through the holidays can feel dishonest and draining for both of you. If you're still unsure, the holidays aren't a reason to rush.

Is it wrong to break up with someone you still love?

No. You can love someone and still not be right together, for example if you want very different lives. Loving someone and choosing to stay are related, but they're not the same thing.

What should I say when I break up with someone?

Keep it short, clear and kind.

A note on safety

If your partner hurts you, threatens you, controls your money or who you see, or makes you feel afraid, please reach out to a local helpline or support service before you end the relationship. Leaving can be the riskiest moment, and support services can help you plan it safely.
