Relationship advice: 7 communication habits that change how you argue

5 Oct 2026 · 3 min read

Most relationship advice sounds obvious until you're in the middle of an argument. Then the obvious parts (stay calm, listen, don't say the thing you'll regret) turn out to be the hardest. The good news is that communicating well is a skill, and skills improve through small, specific habits rather than big resolutions.

These seven come up again and again in the methods therapists use, including the Gottman Method and Nonviolent Communication.

1. Start soft

How a conversation begins usually decides how it ends. John and Julie Gottman found that a harsh opening ("You never help with anything") very rarely leads anywhere good. A softened start-up does the opposite. It says how you feel and what you'd like, without an accusation.

Instead of "You always ignore me when you're on your phone," try "I've been missing time with you lately. Could we put our phones away after dinner?"

2. Describe, don't diagnose

"You're being selfish" is a verdict. "You said you'd be home at six and it's now eight" is an observation. Nonviolent Communication starts with observations because it's hard to argue with a fact and very easy to argue with a label.

3. Name the need underneath

Behind most complaints sits a need: to feel respected, supported, close or free. "I'm furious the dishes are still there" often means "I need to know I'm not carrying this alone." Once you say the need out loud, your partner has something they can actually respond to.

4. Make one clear request

Complaints are easy to wave away, and requests are easy to answer. Swap "You need to be more present" for "Can we have thirty minutes without screens tonight?" A good request is specific, doable and positive. It says what you'd like, not what you want to stop.

5. Listen to understand, not to reply

In a tense moment, try repeating back what you heard before you respond. "So you've had two weeks of late work, and the chores felt like one more demand. Is that right?" You don't have to agree. You only have to show you understood. Most arguments soften once both people feel heard.

6. Take a break when you're flooded

When your heart is pounding and your thoughts are racing, a productive conversation is close to impossible. The Gottmans call this flooding. Pushing through doesn't help. Pausing for at least twenty minutes, doing something calming and then coming back does. What matters is saying you're coming back: "I need twenty minutes to calm down, and then I want to finish this."

7. Repair early and often

Even couples who communicate well say the wrong thing sometimes. What sets them apart is the repair attempt: a joke at the right moment, a gentle touch, "Can we start that over?" or a plain "Sorry, that came out wrong." Repairs work best when they happen quickly and when the other person accepts them.

Where to start

Don't try all seven at once. Pick one, maybe the soft start, and use it for a week. If you want to practice before a real conversation, writing down what you plan to say, or saying it out loud first, can make a bigger difference than you'd expect.

If your conversations regularly become frightening, cruel or controlling, that's a different situation from ordinary conflict. Please reach out to a professional or a support service in your country.
