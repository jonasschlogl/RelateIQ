Most relationship advice sounds obvious until you're in the middle of an argument. Then the obvious parts (stay calm, listen, don't say the thing you'll regret) turn out to be the hardest. The good news is that communicating well is a skill, and skills improve through small, specific habits rather than big resolutions.

These seven habits come up again and again in the methods couples therapists use, especially the Gottman Method and Nonviolent Communication. For each one, you'll find what the research says, why it works and what it sounds like in a real conversation.

What decades of couples research found

Psychologist John Gottman and his colleagues spent decades filming couples as they discussed disagreements, measuring everything from their words to their heart rates. A few findings shape almost all the advice below:

  • The start decides a lot. In Gottman's research, the first three minutes of a conversation predicted how it would end 96% of the time.
  • Conflict itself isn't the problem. Stable couples argued too. During conflict they had about five positive interactions (a smile, a joke, a moment of agreement, an "I see your point") for every negative one. Couples heading for divorce had far fewer. Using this kind of data, Gottman and Robert Levenson predicted which couples would divorce with over 90% accuracy.
  • Most arguments never fully go away. According to the Gottman Institute, 69% of relationship conflicts are about "perpetual problems", ongoing differences in personality, values or needs. Happy couples don't solve them. They learn to talk about them without hurting each other.
  • Small moments count. In a study that followed newlyweds for six years, couples who stayed married had "turned toward" each other's small bids for attention 86% of the time. Couples who divorced had done so only 33% of the time.

1. Start soft

How a conversation begins usually decides how it ends. Gottman's research found that a "harsh start-up", opening with criticism or sarcasm, almost always leads to a conversation that ends badly. A softened start-up does the opposite. It says how you feel and what you'd like, without an accusation.

A simple formula: I feel (emotion) about (specific situation) and I need (positive request).

Instead of

“You always ignore me when you're on your phone.”

Try saying

“I've been missing time with you lately. Could we put our phones away after dinner?”

Why it works

A harsh start puts the other person on defence before they've heard what you actually want. Once someone feels attacked, their energy goes into protecting themselves, not into understanding you. A soft start keeps both of you in the same team for long enough to talk about the real issue. It isn't about being nice instead of honest. It's about being honest in a way the other person can actually hear.

2. Describe, don't diagnose

"You're being selfish" is a verdict. "You said you'd be home at six and it's now eight" is an observation. Nonviolent Communication, the method developed by psychologist Marshall Rosenberg, starts with observations because it's hard to argue with a fact and very easy to argue with a label.

This also avoids what Gottman calls criticism: attacking your partner's character ("You're so lazy") rather than complaining about a specific behaviour ("The bins didn't go out again"). A complaint can be fixed. A character attack can only be defended.

3. Name the need underneath

Behind most complaints sits a need: to feel respected, supported, close or free. "I'm furious the dishes are still there" often means "I need to know I'm not carrying this alone." Once you say the need out loud, your partner has something they can actually respond to.

Example: the dishes argument that wasn't about dishes

Every few days, Eva and Lukas argue about the kitchen. Eva says he never cleans up. Lukas lists the times he did. It goes nowhere.

One evening Eva tries something different: "I think the dishes are just the part I can see. What I really need is to feel that we're running this house together, because lately I feel like the manager." Lukas stops defending and asks what would help. They split the evenings, and the dishes stop being a topic.

4. Make one clear request

Complaints are easy to wave away, and requests are easy to answer. Swap "You need to be more present" for "Can we have thirty minutes without screens tonight?"

A good request is:

  • Specific: your partner knows exactly what you're asking.
  • Doable: it can happen this week, not "from now on, forever".
  • Positive: it says what you'd like, not what you want them to stop.

Bring one request per conversation. Three requests at once sound like a list of failures.

5. Listen to understand, not to reply

In a tense moment, try repeating back what you heard before you respond. "So you've had two weeks of late work, and the chores felt like one more demand. Is that right?" You don't have to agree. You only have to show you understood. Most arguments soften once both people feel heard.

This is also the antidote to defensiveness, another of Gottman's "four horsemen". Defensiveness ("That's not true, you're the one who...") tells your partner their concern doesn't count. Taking responsibility for even a small part ("You're right, I did forget to call") changes the temperature of the conversation.

Try saying

“Before I answer, let me check I've got it. You felt like I didn't take your plans seriously?”

6. Take a break when you're flooded

When your heart is pounding and your thoughts are racing, a productive conversation is close to impossible. Gottman calls this flooding. In his research, flooded partners' heart rates went above 100 beats per minute, sometimes as high as 165. At that point the body is in fight-or-flight mode and listening becomes very hard.

Pushing through doesn't help. Pausing for at least twenty minutes, doing something calming (not rehearsing your arguments) and then coming back does. What matters most is saying you're coming back.

Try saying

“I need twenty minutes to calm down, and then I want to finish this.”

Why it works

Without the promise to return, a break looks exactly like stonewalling, the fourth of Gottman's "four horsemen", where one partner shuts down and withdraws. With the promise, the same break becomes an act of care: "I want to have this conversation well." The person who stays behind can relax because they know the conversation isn't over, and the person who leaves can actually calm down instead of feeling chased.

7. Repair early and often

Even couples who communicate well say the wrong thing sometimes. What sets them apart is the repair attempt: a joke at the right moment, a gentle touch, "Can we start that over?" or a plain "Sorry, that came out wrong." Gottman found that when repair attempts keep failing, it's an accurate marker of an unhappy future. So repairs are worth taking seriously, both making them and accepting them.

Repairs work best when they happen quickly and when the other person accepts them, even if the repair is a little clumsy.

Example: a repair in the middle of an argument

Halfway through a tense talk about holiday plans, Sofia snaps: "You always do what your mum wants." She sees Martin's face drop. Instead of carrying on, she stops: "Wait. That was unfair, and it's not what I mean. Can I try again?" Martin takes a breath and nods. The second version ("I'd like us to decide together before we tell your family") leads to an actual plan.

The four horsemen and their antidotes

Gottman's research identified four communication patterns that reliably predict relationship breakdown. Each one has an antidote, and you'll recognise several of the seven habits above.

The Gottman Institute describes contempt as the single biggest predictor of divorce. If you notice it creeping in, it's worth paying attention to early.

Beyond arguments: two everyday habits

The way you handle conflict depends a lot on what happens between conflicts.

Turn toward small bids. A "bid" is any small attempt to connect: "Look at this", a sigh, a question about your day. Turning toward it (looking up, answering, showing interest) builds trust. In Gottman's six-year follow-up of newlyweds, this was one of the clearest differences between couples who stayed together and those who divorced.

Respond to good news with real enthusiasm. Psychologist Shelly Gable and her colleagues ran four studies on what happens when people share good news with a partner. When partners responded actively and enthusiastically ("That's amazing, tell me everything!") rather than flatly ("Nice") or by changing the subject, couples reported higher relationship well-being, including more intimacy and greater satisfaction.

Try saying

“Wait, you got it? That's huge. How did they tell you?”

Where to start

Don't try all seven at once. Pick one, maybe the soft start, and use it for a week. If you want to practise before a real conversation, writing down what you plan to say, or saying it out loud first, can make a bigger difference than you'd expect.

Quick answers

What is the most important communication skill in a relationship?

If you only work on one, make it the soft start: begin with how you feel and what you'd like, not with a complaint about your partner. In Gottman's research, the first three minutes predicted how a conversation ended 96% of the time.

How do you talk about problems without fighting?

Pick a calm moment, talk about one thing at a time, and end with a specific request. If either of you gets flooded, pause and agree when you'll come back to it.

Is it normal to keep having the same argument?

Yes. According to the Gottman Institute, about 69% of relationship conflicts are about perpetual problems that come from lasting differences. The goal is to discuss them kindly, not to make them disappear. Our guide to relationship problems that keep coming back goes deeper.

What if my partner doesn't want to talk?

Ask for a time rather than a talk right now: "Can we find twenty minutes this weekend to talk about how we split the chores?" A clear, short, scheduled conversation feels much less threatening than an open-ended one.

How can I communicate better with my partner over text?

Keep text for logistics and warmth, and move anything tense to a call or a face-to-face talk. Tone gets lost in writing, so a short "Can we talk about this tonight?" is usually better than a long message.

What is a soft start-up?

It's a way to open a hard conversation by describing how you feel about a specific situation and asking for something positive, instead of starting with criticism.

“I'm stressed about the bills. Could we look at them together on Sunday?”

How do I stop getting defensive?

Notice the urge to explain or counter-attack and look for the part of the complaint that's true, even if it's small. Saying "You're right, I did forget" first usually makes the rest of the conversation much easier.

A note on safety

These habits are for ordinary conflict between two people who both feel safe. If your conversations regularly become frightening, cruel or controlling, that's a different situation. Please reach out to a professional or a support service in your country.
